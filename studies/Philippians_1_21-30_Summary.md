# Philippians 1:21–30 — One-Page Summary

*CSB and NA28. Hansen (PNTC), Fee (NICNT), Hawthorne & Martin (WBC 43), Melick (NAC 32), LRC.
Worksheet method: Charles Simeon Trust, Worksheet Preparation Guide. Audience assumed: a mixed Sunday
congregation.*

## 1 · Structure

**I. The dilemma (21–24)** — maxim (21); living means fruitful labour (22); the two desires, depart or
remain (23–24).
**II. The resolution (25–26)** — he will remain, for their progress and joy, so their boasting overflows.
**III. The charge (27–30)** — one imperative (27a); three marks (27b–28a); the sign (28b); the ground:
believing and suffering both granted (29–30).

**How I found it.** In Greek 1:27–30 is a single sentence with one main verb, `πολιτεύεσθε` — so the
three "marks" are not three commands but one unpacked. `Μόνον` pivots at v27. Word chains: Χριστός
(21, 23, 26, 27, 29), εὐαγγέλιον twice in v27. `συναθλέω` occurs in the NT only here and 4:3, where
Euodia and Syntyche are named, which shows what the imperative is aimed at.

**Emphasis.** A life, and a church, wholly determined by the gospel — in living, in dying and in
suffering. Paul resolves his dilemma not by preference but by usefulness to them, and that move becomes
the pattern for the church.

## 2 · Context that changes the meaning

Philippi was a Roman colony, proud of its citizenship. `πολιτεύεσθε` is a civic word, used in the NT only
here and Acts 23:1; Paul's usual verb is "walk." Fee's gloss: live in the Roman colony as citizens of a
heavenly homeland. The church is a colony inside a colony. The military and athletic images are
sustained — standing a post, contending as a team, horses shying in battle, the arena in v30.

Paul writes in chains facing a capital trial (1:7, 13, 20–23). The Philippians saw his beating and
imprisonment themselves (Acts 16; 1 Thess 2:2) and now face the same pressure.

Literary placement decides the sermon's weight: 1:12–26 is report, 1:27–2:18 is exhortation with the
Christ hymn at its centre. Garland calls 1:27–30 the thesis of the letter; Wright calls it the main
appeal.

## 3 · Author's main point

**Because Christ is gain whether Paul lives or dies, the Philippians must stand together as citizens
worthy of the gospel, unafraid of those who oppose them.**

## 4 · Gospel connection

The command is not a rule but a measure — *worthy of the gospel* — which is why the story of Christ
(2:6–11) sits at the centre of the section it opens. The deepest link is v28b: the same evidence reads
as destruction outside the church and salvation inside it. That is the logic of the cross. Verse 29 then
says believing *and* suffering are both granted as grace. It functions as warrant (Christ's obedience
sets the standard), as power (the one Spirit; "this is from God"), and as reinterpretation (apparent
defeat is readable as victory because it already was once).

## 5 · My main point

**Christ is gain either way, so stand together and fear nothing.**

## 6 · Title and outline

**Gain Either Way**
1. Paul can't lose (21–24) · 2. So he chooses you (25–26) · 3. Live like the colony you are (27–28a) ·
4. The sign that looks like defeat (28b) — *evangelistic turn* · 5. The gift you didn't ask for (29–30)

## 7 · Application

**Christians:** unity is commanded, not atmospheric — the one parallel to `συναθλέω` is 4:3, where two
named women cannot agree (after pt 3). Costly obedience is grace, not misfortune (after pt 5). Test
progress by joy (after pt 2). **Non-Christians:** the sign is readable two ways, and the cross set the
pattern (pt 4). Faith is said to be *granted* — an invitation, not a barrier (pt 5).

## The disagreements that actually matter

**1. The sign in 1:28b.** Your CSB says the opponents see a sign of *their own* destruction. Hansen asks
why they would, calls the psychological explanations speculative, and says the fallback — a sign whether
they recognise it or not — empties the word *sign*. Hawthorne proposed, and Fowl and Hansen follow, that
the opponents read it as a sign of the *Christians'* destruction while believers read the same thing as
salvation. Hawthorne and Martin concede their reading has not won general endorsement; O'Brien and Fee
reject their grammar; O'Brien thinks there is no problem here at all. **Preachable either way, but the
minority reading is far stronger pastorally and you will be departing from the version in their hands.**

**2. "In one Spirit" (1:27).** Fee, Thielman and Hansen take it as the Holy Spirit; Hawthorne and Martin,
with Melick, take it as a common human spirit; O'Brien is ambivalent. Fee's strongest point is that
"one spirit" for a community mood has no analogy in Paul or in Greek, while "one soul" is common. Hansen
attacks the premise instead, calling the assumed synonymous parallelism unwarranted. **What turns on it:
whether unity is a gift to receive or an achievement to produce.**

**3. Where the weight falls.** Hansen, Fee and Melick all start a new major division at 1:27, and LRC
reports a majority closing the unit at 1:26. But Garland and Wright make 1:27–30 the thesis and main
appeal of the whole letter. **Since you are preaching across the seam, the second half should carry the
sermon and 21–26 should function as Paul's worked example.**

**4. "To die is gain" (1:21).** Hawthorne hears the classical note of death as relief from suffering.
Hansen rejects it — Paul insists throughout that his painful life is a joyful one — and reads the gain
as Christ himself, the same verb as 3:8. Martin adds the gain to the gospel from a fearless martyrdom.
**Decides whether v21 is resignation or desire.**
