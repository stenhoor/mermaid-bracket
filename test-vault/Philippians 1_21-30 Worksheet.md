## 1. How has the author organized this passage?

### a) The structure, in sections

**Text type: discourse.** So the structure is the logic.

| Section | Verses | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **I. The dilemma: Christ is gain either way** | **1:21–24** | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;1. The maxim | 1:21 | Living and dying set side by side as the two possibilities |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;2. The first option weighed | 1:22 | Living in the flesh means fruitful labour; Paul cannot say which to choose |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;3. The two desires named | 1:23–24 | Depart and be with Christ (far better) **or** remain in the flesh (more necessary for you) |
| **II. The resolution: he will remain** | **1:25–26** | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;4. The conviction | 1:25 | Persuaded of this, he knows he will remain and continue with all of them |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;5. The purpose chain | 1:25b–26 | For their progress and joy in the faith → so that their boasting in Christ may overflow |
| **III. The charge: one command for a colony under pressure** | **1:27–30** | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;6. The imperative | 1:27a | Live as citizens worthy of the gospel of Christ |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;7. Three marks of that life | 1:27b–28a | Standing firm in one Spirit; contending together as one for the faith of the gospel; unafraid of the opponents |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;8. The sign | 1:28b | Destruction to them, salvation to you — and this from God |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;9. The ground | 1:29–30 | Believing **and** suffering are both granted; the same struggle as Paul's |

The passage straddles a seam. On almost every modern outline, 1:21–26 closes Paul's report of his own
circumstances and 1:27 opens the letter's exhortation. Hansen heads 1:12–26 "Reports of Gospel Ministry"
and starts a new main division at 1:27. Fee begins a new Part of his commentary at 1:27. Melick begins
"Exhortation to Christlike Character (1:27–2:18)" there. Preaching 1:21–30 therefore means preaching
across a hinge, and the sermon has to say what holds the two halves together. The text itself says it:
the gospel of Christ.

### b) Strategies used to find it

1. **Counting main verbs.** This is the single most useful observation, and two commentaries make it
   independently. Hawthorne and Martin note that in Greek 1:27–30 is one sentence with one main verb,
   `πολιτεύεσθε`. Hansen says the same, adding that 2:1–4 is likewise one sentence. Everything in
   1:27–30 after the imperative is participial or subordinate, so the three "marks" are not three
   commands but the unpacking of one.
2. **Tracing the connectives.** `γάρ` (21) ties back to v20; the three `δέ` clauses (22, 23, 24) track
   the alternatives; `καί … πεποιθώς` (25) resolves them; `Μόνον` (27) pivots. Melick notes that `Μόνον`
   itself marks the change of direction, comparing Galatians 2:7–9.
3. **Bracketing the logical relationships.** Diagramming the propositions with the eighteen
   relationships shows a cascade of inferences through 21–26 (premise → dilemma → resolution) and then
   a single Action-Purpose chain hanging off the imperative in 27–30.
4. **Word chains.** `Χριστός` runs through vv21, 23, 26, 27, 29. `ζῆν` / `σάρξ` bind 21–24. `εὐαγγέλιον`
   appears twice in v27 and governs the whole charge. `ἀγών` in v30 picks up Paul's own situation from
   1:12–26.
5. **Reading the whole letter for the repetitions.** `συναθλέω` occurs in the New Testament only at 1:27
   and 4:3 — the second of which names Euodia and Syntyche. `στήκετε` returns at 4:1. `πολιτεύεσθε`
   pairs with `πολίτευμα` at 3:20. Fee argues that 4:2–3 shows what the imperative of 1:27 is actually
   aimed at.
6. **Looking for the join across the seam.** Three independent links hold 21–26 to 27–30 and they are
   what justify preaching them together:
   - Hansen observes that 1:29's threefold "on behalf of Christ / on him / for him" is the corporate
     form of 1:21: salvation is Christ, and to live is Christ.
   - Fee says that having told them his own affairs are all about Christ being glorified, Paul now urges
     the same on them: that for them too, to live is Christ.
   - Fee also ties the "progress and joy in the faith" of v25 directly to contending "for the faith of
     the gospel" in v27, and the "all of you" of v25 to the unity theme of v27.

### c) The emphasis

**A life — and a church — wholly determined by the gospel, in living, in dying and in suffering.**

Paul does not resolve his dilemma by deciding which outcome he prefers. He resolves it by asking which
serves them. That move is the emphasis: the gospel reorders what counts as gain. It then becomes the
pattern for the church. What Paul does with his own life and death in 21–26, he asks them to do with
their unity and their fear in 27–30.

## 2. How does the context inform the meaning?

### a) Literary context

The passage sits at the join of the letter's two halves. 1:12–26 reports that Paul's chains have served
to advance the gospel; 1:27–2:18 is the exhortation that follows, with the Christ hymn of 2:6–11 at its
centre. Hansen's point is that this placement is not accidental: the imperatives of 1:27 onward rest on
the indicative of who Christ is and what he has done, which is why the narrative of Christ sits in the
middle of the ethical section.

Two further observations bear on how much weight 1:27–30 should carry:

- Garland argues that 1:27–30 is more than a bridge into 2:1–4, and that the unit states the thesis of
  Philippians: resist the opponents by becoming one body.
- Wright calls 1:27–30 the main appeal of the letter.

If that is right, then in a passage running 21–30 the weight falls on the second half, and 21–26
functions as Paul's own worked example. Thielman makes the same move from the other direction, reading
Paul's self-presentation in chapter 1 as an example of conduct worthy of the gospel.

Forward links the hearer should be shown: 2:1–4 (the `οὖν` picks up 1:27–30 and turns the unity outward
to those who divide the church); 2:6–11; 3:20 (`πολίτευμα`); 4:1 (`στήκετε` again); 4:2–3 (`συναθλέω`
again, with names attached).

### b) Historical context

Paul writes in chains (1:7, 13, 17), facing a capital trial whose outcome he does not know (1:20–23).
That is not a rhetorical pose; the whole dilemma depends on execution being a live possibility. The
Philippians are themselves under pressure from opponents (1:28) and are undergoing the same struggle
they once saw Paul in (1:30).

What they saw is in Acts 16:16–40: Paul and Silas seized, dragged before the magistrates, accused of
advocating customs unlawful for Romans, stripped, beaten and jailed. Paul himself refers to it in
1 Thessalonians 2:2. What they have since heard is in the letter: his chains, the imperial guard (1:13),
and believers in Caesar's household (4:22).

The provenance of the letter (Rome, Ephesus or Caesarea) is disputed. Hansen's conclusion is the useful
one for preaching: Paul's travel plans can be explained on any of the three, so the question has to be
settled on other grounds and nothing in this passage turns on it.

### c) Cultural context

This is where the passage is most easily flattened in English, and where the congregation needs help.

- **Philippi was a Roman colony.** Octavian settled veterans there after the battle fought on its plains,
  and the city took visible pride in its Roman status. Its people held Roman citizenship.
- **`πολιτεύεσθε` is a civic word, not a generic one.** It occurs in the New Testament only here and in
  Acts 23:1. Paul's ordinary verb for conduct is `περιπατέω`, "walk"; Fee and Melick both stress that he
  has deliberately chosen a different one. It means to live out your life as a citizen, discharging
  public obligations. Fee's gloss is the sharpest: live in the Roman colony of Philippi as worthy
  citizens of your heavenly homeland. The church is a colony inside a colony.
- **The imperial cult.** Fee argues that the loyalty of the colony to the emperor's cult is the most
  likely source of the pressure: a town that honoured the emperor at every public gathering would notice
  a group whose allegiance had been transferred to another `κύριος`, one executed by the empire.
- **The military and athletic images are deliberate and sustained.** `στήκετε` is the soldier who will
  not leave his post. `συναθλοῦντες` shifts the picture to athletes contending as a team, side by side.
  `μὴ πτυρόμενοι` is a rare word used of horses shying in battle. `ἀγών` in v30 is the arena.
- **`ἀναλῦσαι` in v23** is a common euphemism for death, drawn from a ship casting off or an army
  striking camp. Hansen's caution is worth repeating in the pulpit: the word does not import a Platonic
  soul-leaving-body metaphysic, and the context does not warrant reading one in.
- **Martyrdom literature.** Hansen shows that 4 Maccabees uses `ἀγών` of Jewish martyrs under Antiochus
  in explicit comparison with athletes — the contest of the athlete and the torture of the martyr
  happening in the same arena before the same crowd. That is the conceptual world of v30.

### d) Biblical context

The guide restricts this to the author's own use of earlier scripture, and this passage is thin on
citation. One item matters and it is just upstream of our text.

- **Philippians 1:19 appears to quote Job 13:16 LXX verbatim** — "this will turn out for my
  deliverance/salvation." If so, Paul casts himself in Job's position: a righteous sufferer who expects
  vindication, speaking before a tribunal. That colours `σωτηρία` when it reappears in 1:28 and supports
  Hansen's argument that salvation there is present as well as future. **Caveat: I have not verified
  this against the LXX in this session; confirm it in your Greek tab before using it.**
- **The `πόλις` background.** Hawthorne and Martin argue that Paul's civic language draws on the Jewish
  hope of the city of the great king and the commonwealth of Israel into which the nations would come
  (Ps 48:2; Ps 87; Isa 66:20 LXX; Amos 9:11–12; Zech 14:8–11), so that `πολιτεύεσθε` carries both a
  Greco-Roman and a scriptural charge. This reading is contested — see §8.9 below.

## 3. The author's main point to his audience (one sentence)

> **Because Christ is gain whether Paul lives or dies, the Philippians must stand together as citizens
> worthy of the gospel, unafraid of those who oppose them.**

Shorter form, if you want it tighter: *Christ is gain either way, so stand together and fear nothing.*

It is a call to action, which the guide permits. The content of the call is corporate, not individual:
every verb in 1:27–30 that describes the church is plural, and the one command is about how a community
conducts itself in public.

## 4. How does this passage connect to the gospel of Jesus Christ?

### The connection

Three strands, all organic to the wording, running from the most direct to the most structural. The
Guide's categories in play are **Direct Reference**, **Ethic of the Gospel**, and participation.

1. **Direct reference.** The gospel of Christ is named as the norm (v27), and the faith of the gospel is
   what they contend for. Christ is the content of life and the content of death (vv21, 23).
2. **The ethic of the gospel.** The imperative is not a rule but a measure: *worthy of the gospel*.
   Hansen puts it well — Paul does not impose a list; he sets out a person. That is why the story of
   Christ in 2:6–11 stands at the centre of the section the imperative opens. The imperatives rest on
   the indicatives.
3. **The cross as the key to the sign.** This is the deepest connection and it is in v28b. The same
   outward fact — a community that will not back down and is suffering for it — reads as destruction to
   those outside and salvation to those inside. That is exactly the logic of the cross. Hansen draws the
   line explicitly: to unbelievers the cross is simply a sign of destruction; to believers it is the
   sign of salvation. Paul then says in v29 that both believing and suffering are granted as grace, on
   behalf of Christ, and in 2:8–9 that the one who was obedient to the point of death was for that
   reason exalted.

### How it functions in relation to the main point

It supplies the warrant and the power, and it reinterprets the evidence.

- **Warrant.** "Worthy of the gospel" only means something if the gospel has a shape. Christ's
  self-humbling obedience gives the standard.
- **Power.** The text refuses to let the command rest on the congregation's resolve. They stand firm *in
  the one Spirit*; the whole thing is *from God* (v28b); even their suffering is *granted* (v29).
- **Reinterpretation.** The gospel connection answers the objection the passage raises. If standing firm
  produces suffering, how is that salvation? Because that is what happened to Christ. The cross is the
  precedent that makes apparent defeat readable as victory.

### Why it matters for this audience

For the believer it addresses the fear that suffering is evidence of God's displeasure or absence. The
text says the opposite: it is grace, it is granted, and it is the same struggle the apostle was in.
For the unbeliever it puts the real question — not "is Christianity useful?" but "is Christ worth
dying for?" Paul's answer is that he is worth both living and dying for, and the sign that looks like
destruction is the only one worth betting on.

## 5. The main point I will argue to my audience (one sentence)

> **Christ is gain either way, so stand together and fear nothing.**

Ten words. It carries the author's point forward and lets the gospel connection do the work: *either way*
is only true because of the cross and resurrection, and *fear nothing* is only possible because the sign
has already been read correctly once, at Golgotha.

## 6. Sermon title and preaching outline

**Title:** *Gain Either Way*
Alternative, if you want the civic image in the title: *A Colony Under Pressure*

**Preaching outline** (tracking the structure of §1, with the gospel connection carried at point 3):

1. **Paul can't lose** (1:21–24)
   Both doors open onto Christ. The dilemma is real, and that is the point.
2. **So he chooses you** (1:25–26)
   The tie-breaker is not preference but usefulness. Progress and joy in the faith.
3. **Now you: live like the colony you are** (1:27–28a)
   One command, three marks. Standing firm, contending as one, unafraid.
4. **The sign that looks like defeat** (1:28b)
   The same evidence read two ways. The cross as the precedent. *Evangelistic turn here.*
5. **The gift you didn't ask for** (1:29–30)
   Believing and suffering, both granted. The same struggle.

## 7. Application

**For Christians**

- **Unity is the command, not the atmosphere** (after point 3). The verbs are plural and the one New
  Testament parallel to `συναθλέω` is 4:3, where Paul names two women who had contended at his side and
  now cannot agree. Make it concrete: the congregation's witness in this town is carried by whether it
  can hold together under pressure, not by its individual convictions.
- **Reread your costly obedience** (after point 5). Public identification with Christ costs something
  here too, even without persecution: in what you will not say at work, what you will not laugh at, who
  you will not drop. The text calls that grace, not misfortune. Hansen's limit is worth keeping — this
  is suffering caused by identification with Christ, not everyday hardship, and the sermon should not
  blur the two.
- **Test your progress by your joy** (after point 2). Hansen's line is usable from the pulpit: progress
  without joy is spurious, joy without progress is counterfeit.
- **Hold your plans the way Paul held his** (after point 2). He sets his own preference aside for the
  church's usefulness. That is an application for anyone making a decision about where to live, what to
  take, when to retire.

**For non-Christians**

- **The sign is readable two ways** (at point 4, the evangelistic turn). Everyone in the room is already
  reading the evidence. A church that suffers and does not quit either looks foolish or looks like
  people who know something. Paul says the same event bears both readings, and the cross is where that
  pattern was set.
- **Put the real question** (at point 1). Paul's confidence is not that nothing bad will happen. It is
  that neither outcome can take Christ from him. Ask what in your life survives both outcomes.
- **Name the gift** (at point 5). Faith itself is said to be granted. That is an invitation, not a
  barrier: if believing is a gift, it can be asked for.

## 8. Where the commentators disagree, and the major questions

Restricted to Philippians 1:21–30. Each entry gives the options, who holds them, and what turns on it.

### 8.1 Where does the unit divide? *(the question your passage choice raises)*
- **1:27–30 closes chapter 1.** Anders; Hendriksen.
- **1:12–26 is the unit; 1:27 opens a new major section.** The majority, represented by Reumann; so also
  Hansen (new division at 1:27), Fee (new Part at 1:27), Melick ("Exhortation to Christlike Character,
  1:27–2:18").
- **Hybrid, facing both ways.** Melick is reported by LRC as treating 1:27–30 as both the conclusion of
  the preceding material and the opening of the exhortation.
- **1:27–2:11 as one unit.** Silva reads 2:5–11 as the conclusion of what began at 1:27.
- **1:27–30 as the thesis of the letter.** Garland; Wright calls it the main appeal.
- *What turns on it:* whether your sermon's weight falls on Paul's example (21–26) or on the charge
  (27–30). The thesis reading argues for the charge.

### 8.2 "To die is gain" (1:21) — gain of what?
- **Christological.** The gain is Christ himself; the same verb appears at 3:8 ("that I may gain
  Christ"), and v23 says what the gain is. So Hansen.
- **Hellenistic relief from suffering.** Hawthorne reads it against the classical use of the word for
  the release death brings, citing Sophocles, and calls them the words of a very human Paul voicing a
  universal sentiment.
- **Missional.** Martin adds that the gain includes the promotion of the gospel through a fearless
  martyrdom, not only Paul's own reward.
- Hansen rejects the Hellenistic reading on the ground that Paul insists throughout the letter that his
  painful life is a joyful one, and sides with Martin. Note the oddity: Hawthorne and Martin are
  co-authors of the WBC volume, and Hansen takes one against the other.

### 8.3 `οὐ γνωρίζω` (1:22) — "I do not know" or "I cannot make known"?
- **"I do not know"** — effectively all translations, including CSB.
- **"I cannot make known"** — Hansen, on the ground that all seventeen other Pauline uses of the verb
  mean "make known, reveal," and that translations make an unexplained exception here.
- *What turns on it:* whether v22c reports Paul's inner uncertainty or his refusal to declare a
  preference. Minor for the sermon, but it is a real minority reading and worth knowing you are
  departing from it.

### 8.4 Does Paul have a choice at all? (1:22)
Hansen says the question is rhetorical: Paul neither has the power to choose nor is deciding which to
petition God for. Others read a genuine deliberation. The text's `τί αἱρήσομαι` will bear either.

### 8.5 "Depart and be with Christ" (1:23) — what is being claimed?
- **The standard reading:** conscious fellowship with Christ immediately after death, with 2 Corinthians
  5:8 and 1 Thessalonians 4:17 as the controlling parallels. This generates the classic puzzle of how
  communion after death relates to communion at the parousia.
- **Hansen's reading:** 1 Thessalonians 4:17 is not a close parallel because the contexts differ — here
  Paul is thinking about execution, not the parousia. He makes 3:10 the controlling text and argues Paul
  is speaking of being with Christ *in* death, not only after it, so that the phrase connects forward to
  1:29–30.
- **The intermediate state itself** — conscious bodiless fellowship, soul-sleep, or immediate
  resurrection in the eternal realm — Hansen lists as genuinely open and long debated.
- *What turns on it:* whether 1:23 is primarily about the afterlife (and so a comfort text at funerals)
  or primarily about union with Christ in dying (and so a bridge to vv29–30). It also decides how much
  of the sermon goes to the intermediate state, which the text does not actually settle.

### 8.6 How can Paul "know" he will remain? (1:25)
He has just said he does not know which to choose. Hansen resolves it developmentally: having weighed
the benefits and seen the church's need in v24, the question was answered and he became convinced, so
`πεποιθὼς οἶδα` is an inference, not a revelation. Others take it as settled confidence or near-prophetic
certainty. The pastoral stakes are real, since Paul may well have been executed.

### 8.7 The two `ἐν` phrases in 1:26
- **First = object of boasting, second = means.** Some interpreters.
- **First = sphere (in union with Christ), second = object of their pride (Paul and his mission).**
  O'Brien, followed in substance by the NEB.
- Fee's formulation reconciles them: where the boast is "in someone," the boast is still in Christ.
  Hansen notes that both lines agree Christ is the ultimate ground and Paul's mission the occasion.

### 8.8 `πολιτεύεσθε` (1:27) — Roman, heavenly, or both?
- **Civic and this-worldly.** Bruce Winter: the focus is the Christian's civic responsibility in the
  public place, and he holds that the cognate in 3:20 should not govern the translation here.
- **Both.** Hawthorne and Martin, explicitly against Gnilka; Hansen follows them, paraphrasing the
  command as being good citizens of Philippi *and* of heaven.
- **Dual, with the heavenly controlling.** Fee: not "live as citizens of Rome," though that is not
  irrelevant, but live in the Roman colony as citizens of a heavenly homeland.
- **Covenantal / Torah background.** Miller, against Brewer, works from LXX and Hellenistic Jewish usage
  where the term concerns covenant obedience, making Christ the new norm in place of Torah. O'Brien
  judges this extended theological sense overdone, though possibly preparing for 3:2–7; Fee is against it
  while insisting on the Roman-colony relevance.
- Translations show the split: the TNIV inserts "heaven," which is not in the Greek; the CSB's "as
  citizens of heaven" does the same. The Greek says only "live as citizens, worthily of the gospel."

### 8.9 `ἐν ἑνὶ πνεύματι` (1:27) — the Holy Spirit or a common human spirit?
This is the most crowded dispute in the passage.
- **The Holy Spirit:** Jones, Dibelius, Bonnard, Martin (in his 1959 work), Gnilka, Collange, Fee,
  Thielman, Hansen.
- **The human or corporate spirit:** Hawthorne and Martin in the revised WBC, Melick, Michaelis,
  Lohmeyer, Beare, Schweizer, Moffatt.
- **Both at once:** Scott, opposed by Fee.
- **Ambivalent:** O'Brien.
- **The WBC case:** the chiastic construction brings "one soul" hard up against "one spirit," so the two
  are equivalent, and nothing signals anything more.
- **Fee's four counters:** "one spirit" for a community disposition has no analogy in Greek, Paul or the
  New Testament, whereas "one soul" is common; Paul's "stand firm in" is invariably locative, which fits
  the Spirit and not a dative of manner; 2:1–4 resumes both words with "participation in the Spirit"; and
  Paul uses this exact phrase of the Holy Spirit in 1 Corinthians 12:13 and Ephesians 2:18, precisely
  where the one Spirit grounds unity.
- **Hansen's counter** goes at the premise rather than the conclusion: that the parallelism exists and is
  synonymous is, he says, an unwarranted assumption. He adds 4:1 ("stand firm in the Lord") as the nearest
  parallel and 1:19 and 2:1 from the immediate context.
- Note the irony worth a footnote in your own notes: Martin argued for the Holy Spirit in 1959 and the
  revised volume he co-authored argues against it.
- *What turns on it:* whether unity is a gift to be received or an achievement to be produced. Hansen
  draws the line sharply — unity in the one Spirit is God's gift, unity of one soul is the church's task.

### 8.10 `τῇ πίστει τοῦ εὐαγγελίου` (1:27) — a phrase unique in the New Testament
- **Dative of advantage,** "for the faith," with `πίστις` close to a creedal sense: Hawthorne and Martin,
  with O'Brien, and `τοῦ εὐαγγελίου` as a subjective genitive (the gospel generates the faith).
- **Dative of association** governed by the `συν-` of `συναθλοῦντες`, "struggling together *with* the
  faith": Hall, with Jones and Lightfoot.
- **Appositional genitive,** "the faith, that is, the gospel": Fee.
- **Deliberately unresolved:** Hansen thinks the options are not substantially different and all three
  senses may be present.

### 8.11 Who are the opponents? (1:28)
- **Roman, non-Christian, imperial-cult pressure.** Fee, from the colony's loyalty to the emperor and the
  "same struggle" of v30; Hansen, from the same equation, noting Paul's struggle is defined by chains, the
  guard and possible execution.
- **Non-Christian Jews.** Hawthorne and Martin argue the threat to "the faith of the gospel" points to
  evangelistically fervent Jews resident in Philippi or arrived from Thessalonica, possibly the same
  people as the "dogs" of 3:2 — while conceding there is no definite indication Paul is debating
  Judaizers here. They name Michael, Martin's earlier work, and Loh and Nida as holding the pagan-only
  view they are resisting.
- *What turns on it:* whether the suffering in view is social and political or doctrinal, and therefore
  what you tell your congregation their own version of it looks like.

### 8.12 The sign in 1:28b — the hardest verse in the passage
The Greek is elliptical and something has to be supplied.
- **Majority and most translations, including CSB:** the sign is to the opponents a sign of *their own*
  destruction, and of the Christians' salvation. Lightfoot's reading of the antecedent stands behind this.
- **The objection:** Hansen asks why opponents would read Christian steadfastness as a sign of their own
  ruin. Psychological explanations he calls wildly speculative; the fallback that it is a sign "whether
  they recognise it or not" he says empties the word *sign* of meaning.
- **The alternative:** the sign is recognised by the opponents as a sign of the *Christians'* destruction,
  while the believers read the same thing as their salvation. Hawthorne proposed it, Fowl followed, Hansen
  adopts it. Hawthorne and Martin set it out with the antecedent of `ἥτις` taken as "the faith" of v27
  rather than the fearlessness of v28, and candidly note that their reading "has not commanded general
  endorsement."
- **Dissent on the antecedent:** O'Brien and Fee both reject taking `τῇ πίστει` as the antecedent.
- **The radical option:** Westcott and Hort found the words so difficult that they proposed putting vv28b
  and 29 in parenthesis so that v30 attaches to v28a.
- **And the deflation:** O'Brien finds no problem here needing a solution at all.
- *What turns on it:* whether the sermon says the opponents are unwittingly reading their own doom, or
  that the two communities are reading the same evidence in opposite directions. The second is far more
  preachable and is also the harder reading to defend from the versions your congregation is holding.

### 8.13 Is `σωτηρία` in 1:28 future only?
The CSB and TNIV render it as a future verb ("you will be saved"). Hansen argues for present as well as
future, pointing to 1:19 where the same noun is glossed by courage for Christ to be exalted in his body
whether by life or death. If 1:19 is quoting Job 13:16, that strengthens his case.

### 8.14 How far does the anti-imperial reading go?
Hansen states it strongly: Paul's gospel that Jesus is the exalted Lord directly confronted the imperial
gospel that Caesar is lord and saviour, and both Paul and the Philippians were in the same life-and-death
struggle with Rome. Fee reaches a similar conclusion more cautiously, offering it as a surmise from
hints in the letter. Hawthorne and Martin resist making the opposition purely pagan. This is a live area
of Pauline scholarship and the passage is one of its standard proof-texts, so claim it carefully.

## A note on method

The four commentaries and the LRC were read in the Logos web app. Logos renders book text to a canvas
element, so none of it is extractable as text; it was read visually, page by page. Everything attributed
above was read in this session except where marked "reported by" or listed as cited at second hand.
Quotation has been kept to short phrases; the arguments are summarised rather than reproduced.

Two things to check before you use this:

1. The Job 13:16 allusion at 1:19, flagged in §2(d). I did not verify it against the LXX here.
2. The audience assumption at the head of the worksheet. Questions 5 to 7 depend on it.
